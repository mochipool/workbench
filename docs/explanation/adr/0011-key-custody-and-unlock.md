# ADR 0011: Key custody and unlock

Status: accepted (owner, 2026-11-17). Extends [ADR 0003](./0003-vault-and-store.md) (key slots) and uses the data classes of [ADR 0004](./0004-data-classification.md). Pairs with [ADR 0010](./0010-object-identity.md).

## Amends

* [ADR 0003](./0003-vault-and-store.md), "Keys" diagram: the line "(later: hardware token)" is replaced by the slot kinds of decision 3.
* ADR 0003, "Keys": the sentence "A workspace always has at least one passphrase slot and one recovery slot" is replaced by the policy of decision 6.
* ADR 0003, "Rekey", step 3: "replaces all slots by one passphrase slot and one new recovery slot" is replaced by: a rekey re-creates the slots that the workspace policy allows, and each slot to be re-created needs its factors present at the rekey (decision 3). A slot whose factors are not present is not re-created, the operator is told which, and the rekey is refused if the result would break the policy or leave no slot.
* ADR 0003, "Keys" and "Records": the "workspace id" bound in slot and record associated data is read as the **genesis nonce** (16 random bytes, `seal.IDSize`). A new workspace draws the nonce at creation; `store convert` of an existing workspace sets the nonce to the existing random workspace id, so every existing slot and record keeps valid associated data without re-sealing.
* ADR 0003, "Keys", the associated data of a slot: it also binds the hash of the recovery public key (decision 6).
* ADR 0003, "Backup and recovery": "`spo store recover` unlocks with the recovery key, requires a new passphrase, and rekeys" holds under the `standard` policy. Under `hardware` there is no passphrase slot to replace, so recovery requires re-enrolling the policy's factors (new authenticators and, where a slot is composite, a new passphrase), and the new slots are created with those factors present (decision 3).
* ADR 0003, "Records": the owner name in the associated data, the sealed owner blob and the lookup tag is replaced by the anchor identity of the object's owner; this is stated and migrated in [ADR 0010](./0010-object-identity.md), Amends and decision 4. The sealed format of cold-role records gains a second wrap (decision 6, cold-role sub-keys).
* [ADR 0005](./0005-party-envelope-and-signing-policy.md), "What the signer shows, and re-authentication" and obligation 6: the approval gate that "prompts for the store passphrase" (and "asks for the passphrase again") becomes a fresh presentation of a factor that the workspace policy accepts. Under the `hardware` policy there is no passphrase, so a passphrase prompt could never be satisfied.
* ADR 0005, "Which keys may sign remotely (M6)" and "Which refusals are sent": the cold-key rule is keyed on the key identity's recorded roles, forbids cold-role answers over a network transport only (the file transport stays allowed), and its refusal is silent where it rests on that history; this is stated in [ADR 0010](./0010-object-identity.md), Amends and decision 3. Cold-role keys never sign proof-of-possession challenges. Committee cold keys are refused over a network as well, which ADR 0005's M6 table does not require; that is listed in ADR 0010, Amends.
* [ADR 0002](./0002-architecture-and-dependencies.md), "Layout" and "Third-party modules". Layout: `adapters/upstreamdir` is the read-only upstream source reader (the read side of the upstream layout, the source of `workspace import`); `adapters/exportdir` is the export directory writer; `adapters/fido2` and `adapters/vaultkms` (factor sources and key-wrap backends) are added; `test/plainstore` (the write side of the plain layout, test builds only) is added under `test/`. Modules: the libfido2 command-line tools (a pinned tool, not a module), a Vault HTTP client (no module), and the AWS KMS module (decision 8). Each enters the `confined` table of `spo/internal/archtest/arch_test.go`.

## Context

Requirements from the owner that bind this record: R2 securables such as cold keys are encrypted at rest and no insecure mechanism is tolerated; R3 unlock is stronger than a password (FIDO2, passkeys, Vault backends); R4 a server has no way to decrypt on its own; R5 standard, widely adopted mechanisms.

**What the code does today.** The sealed store wraps one 32-byte master key in key slots, a passphrase slot (Argon2id) and a recovery slot (HKDF over 32 random bytes); `seal.Slot` has the kinds `SlotPassphrase` and `SlotRecovery` only (`spo/internal/platform/seal/slot.go`). A slot's associated data binds format version, workspace id, slot id, kind, KDF description and commitment. Records are sealed under per-record data keys ([ADR 0003](./0003-vault-and-store.md)). That part meets R2.

**Where secrets are written without that protection today.**

| Path | What it does | Code |
| --- | --- | --- |

Signing keys, mnemonics, party identity keys and tailcat node keys are written as upstream writes them, in clear, mode 0400. **What an upstream password is.** Upstream's key envelope is OpenPGP symmetric encryption (SPO-Ckey-INV-003: AES-256, iterated and salted S2K, an integrity packet) over a password that upstream's prompt has already reshaped (SPO-Ckey-SEC-001 to SEC-004, `spec/upstream/common/Ckey.md`). The strength rule is upstream's (10 characters, four classes), well below the Argon2id parameters of ADR 0003.

## Decision

### 1. No secret is written unencrypted by the release binary, except one named delivery

The rule covers signing keys, mnemonics, party and tailcat keys, provider tokens and every other Secret-class record of ADR 0004. It is enforced by types. The release binary has **three** writers of key material, and no other package can obtain a type that writes a Secret kind to a file:

1. the sealed store (encrypted);
2. export to a recipient (`age`, ADR 0005; concern E6), including `node export` of decision 2, and the upstream-compatible encrypted export (all encrypted);
3. the block producer's **start-up helper** (decision 2), which decrypts a `node export` into a systemd credential or a `ramfs` mount on the block producer's host. It is the third writer and the only plaintext writer in the release binary, and it is restricted by type to the KES signing key, the VRF signing key and the operational certificate: no other key type can be passed to it. It also verifies the types of what it decrypts itself: it refuses to write any decrypted artefact that is not, by its text-envelope type, a KES signing key, a VRF signing key or an operational certificate.

A fourth writer, the plain-directory store, exists only in test builds (decision 2). The `archtest` rules check that the only importers of the writer types are these adapters, and they run twice: with the release build tags and with `-tags plainlayout`, so that the one file that carries the tag cannot import what the rule forbids. Walking the command tree is not the enforcement, because a command added later would pass such a walk.

### 2. What happens to each path

* **Reading an upstream plain directory** stays, as an import source only. The read-only reader is `adapters/upstreamdir` (the read-only upstream source reader), which has no method that writes. The flag `--upstream-layout` is removed from the release binary; import takes a directory argument as it does now.
* **The plain-directory store as a working store** is removed from the release binary. Its write side (`Update`, `PutSecret`) is kept for tests only. Many test files used the plain store, among them the parity suite in `spo/test/parity` that compares with the upstream scripts, which work on plain files. The package is split: `adapters/upstreamdir` is the read-only upstream source reader (list, open, subdirs, name mapping), `adapters/exportdir` is the export directory writer (decision 2, `workspace export`), and the write side of the plain layout is `spo/test/plainstore`.
  * **Import rule, stated precisely.** `spo/test/plainstore` may be imported only (a) from `_test.go` files and from `spo/test`, and (b) from one non-test file of `cmd/spo`, the test wiring (`wiring_plain.go`), which carries the build constraint `//go:build plainlayout`. No other package and no file without the constraint may import it; an `archtest` rule checks both parts. The end-to-end tests that drive the compiled binary with `--upstream-layout` (`cmd/spo/main_test.go`, `workspace_e2e_test.go`) need the flag in that binary, so they build it with the tag `plainlayout`; the release derivation in `tools/spo.nix` does not set the tag.
  * **Release check.** The `checkPhase` of that derivation asserts three things. First, `go list -deps` of `./cmd/spo` with the release tags (the tags of the derivation, which do not include `plainlayout`) lists no package `spo/test/plainstore`, and none of the write side that moved there: a package that is not in the build cannot be linked, which is a proof and not a search. Second, `--help` exposes no plain-layout flag or command. Third, as a secondary check, the symbol table (`go tool nm`, run on the binary before stripping, and a search of the function names that survive in the Go runtime tables of the stripped one; the latter is unconfirmed) contains no symbol of those packages. The flag's absence from `--help` alone would pass a binary that still links the writer, and symbol scraping alone can miss inlined code, so the dependency list is the check that decides.
  * The test-only plain store refuses the mainnet network magic.
* **`key unprotect`** is removed with the flag. **`key protect`** is removed too: it operates on the plain-directory store only.
* **`workspace export --plaintext`** is removed. `export --password-fd` writes upstream's encrypted form, which remains available for returning to the upstream scripts (gpg stays external, concern X5). That form inherits upstream's gpg symmetric mode and its weak S2K, so the export requires a passphrase that `spo` generates (not a typed one), or prefers `age` for the path where the recipient is not the upstream scripts.
* **`--keep-encrypted`** becomes `--defer-encrypted`: the encrypted key file is *not* imported, is listed in the report as left in the source, and the import refuses to count its owner as imported. A kept opaque record that cannot sign has no use in the store, and it holds a key that is only as safe as an upstream password. The default stays decrypt and seal (E9). With no password and no terminal the import fails with the owner named, as now.
* **Existing `UpstreamEncryptedSKey` records** (`ports.UpstreamEncryptedSKey`, kind `upstream-encrypted.skey`, Secret class, written by `--keep-encrypted` of today's import in `app/workspace/import.go`). The record holds the gpg-wrapped ciphertext of an upstream key, undecrypted. Under decision 1 it remains allowed only as a sealed record: its payload is sealed like any other Secret payload, and the upstream ciphertext is never written out. Today `workspace export` writes such a record back under its upstream name as an encrypted file (`app/workspace/export.go`, `cli/workspace.go`); that is removed, and an export lists the record as not exportable. Nothing can sign with it as it is: no signer reads it, and there is no signer entry for it in the `SignerDirectory`. It becomes a signing key only through one explicit use case, `key convert <name>`, which asks the operator for the upstream password through the guarded prompt, decrypts the gpg envelope with the external `gpg` (passphrase through an inherited file descriptor, decrypted bytes into guarded memory), checks that the key's verification key hash is the identity the record is bound to, seals the plain key as an ordinary signing-key record, destroys the opaque record in the same transaction and writes an audit event. Nothing is written to disk in clear at any step. No new record of this kind is created after this change.
* **Block-producer delivery (exception; owner decision, open questions).** `cardano-node` reads `kes.skey`, `vrf.skey` and the operational certificate from files in clear. R2 cannot be met literally for the hot KES and VRF keys on the node's host. Proposed: one named use case `node export`, restricted by type to the KES signing key, the VRF signing key and the operational certificate, and never a cold, payment, stake, DRep or committee key or a mnemonic. `node export` writes `age` ciphertext sealed to a recipient key that belongs to the block producer; it is audited and refused unless the certificate's KES verification key matches the KES key delivered. It writes no plaintext. The plaintext writer is the start-up helper below.
  * **The recipient's decryption identity is itself a key on that host.** It is held only as a TPM-bound `systemd-creds` credential (`systemd-creds encrypt` with a TPM 2.0 key; the exact options are unconfirmed) and never as a file in clear. Without a TPM, `systemd-creds` falls back to a key file of the host (unconfirmed), which protects against nothing that can read the disk; `node export` records that the host has no TPM-bound identity and the report says so.
  * **Where the decrypted files go.** The start-up helper, the third plaintext writer of decision 1 and restricted by type as there, decrypts the delivered files into the service's credential directory (`LoadCredentialEncrypted=` or the equivalent) or, failing that, a `ramfs` mount verified with `statfs`, mode 0400 for the node user. `tmpfs` is not accepted, because tmpfs pages can be swapped to disk; `ramfs` cannot. Whether the systemd credentials directory is always `ramfs` or can be `tmpfs` is unconfirmed, and the start-up helper checks the file-system type rather than assuming.
  * **What stays in plaintext, and where.** The KES and VRF signing keys and the certificate, on the credential or `ramfs` mount of the block producer for as long as the service runs, and in the node's memory; the `age` identity, in the helper's memory while it decrypts. Nothing is in clear on a disk. The cold key, the master key and every other secret never reach the host. It is the single place where a signing key is in clear, by necessity: without it a pool cannot be operated.

### 3. Envelope encryption stays; unlock methods are slots

ADR 0003's hierarchy is unchanged: factor, slot key, master key, per-purpose HKDF keys, per-record data keys. A *factor* is a secret the operator holds or a device or service can reproduce. A *slot kind* is a way to obtain a 32-byte slot key from factors; the slot wraps the master key with XChaCha20-Poly1305, stores the commitment, and binds its own parameters in associated data, exactly as the two existing kinds do. The format version rises (a slot of an unknown kind is refused, as today).

**Existing kinds keep ADR 0003's derivation unchanged.** A `passphrase` slot's key is the Argon2id output over the typed bytes with its 16-byte salt and the floors and ceilings of ADR 0003. A `recovery` slot's key is HKDF-SHA-256 over the 32 random bytes, as ADR 0003 specifies. Neither goes through the derivation below.

**New kinds (`fido2-hmac`, `composite`) use one derivation.** The inputs are the factor outputs, each exactly 32 bytes (the `hmac-secret` output; the unwrapped secret B of a Vault or KMS backend; the Argon2id output of a passphrase factor with its own salt and parameters stored in the slot; the 32 bytes of a recovery factor). They are ordered by (factor kind code, factor id) ascending, an order that the slot stores and binds in its associated data. Then:

```
IKM  = for each input i in order: uint32_be(len(input_i)) || input_i
salt = genesis nonce (16 bytes)
info = canonical CBOR of ["spo/slot-key", format version, slot id, slot kind,
                          [[factor kind, factor id], ...] in the same order]
slot key = HKDF-SHA-256(IKM, salt, info), 32 bytes (RFC 5869)
```

The length prefix is a 4-byte big-endian unsigned integer, so no input can be shifted into its neighbour. Workspaces are separated by the salt (a factor reused by mistake in two workspaces gives different slot keys), slots by the slot id in `info`, and factor sets by the kinds and ids in `info`. A `fido2-hmac` slot is the one-input case of the same derivation. The slot's associated data binds format version, genesis nonce, slot id, kind, the ordered factor list, each factor's parameters (credential id, relying-party id and the per-slot salt of decision 4; Vault or KMS key reference; Argon2id parameters), the hash of the recovery public key (decision 6) and the commitment.

| Kind | Slot key | Standard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `passphrase` (exists) | Argon2id as in ADR 0003 | RFC 9106 |
| `recovery` (exists) | HKDF-SHA-256 as in ADR 0003 | RFC 5869 |
| `fido2-hmac` | the derivation above over the 32-byte `hmac-secret` output | CTAP 2.1 `hmac-secret` (decision 4); RFC 5869 |
| `composite` | the derivation above over 2 or more factor outputs (AND) | RFC 5869 |

A Vault or KMS backend is a factor, not a slot kind (decision 5). The commitment (an HMAC under the slot key, checked before the AEAD) is kept for every kind: it is what makes a slot open under exactly one key.

### 4. FIDO2 and passkeys: what applies to unlocking a file

"The full FIDO2 specification" is the WebAuthn API (a browser and a relying-party server) plus CTAP2 (the authenticator protocol). Unlocking a local file uses a small part of it.

* **Applies:** CTAP2 `authenticatorMakeCredential` once at enrolment with the `hmac-secret` and `credProtect` extensions; `authenticatorGetAssertion` with `hmac-secret` at every unlock, passing a 32-byte salt; PIN or built-in user verification (`clientPin`, PIN protocol 1 or 2). The credential is bound to the relying-party id `spo.<genesis nonce in hex>` (32 hex characters). The nonce exists at creation and never changes, so nothing that is written at creation depends on a value derived later. A converted store uses its existing random id as nonce (see Amends). The `hmac-secret` salt sent to the authenticator is 32 random bytes drawn at enrolment, stored in the slot, and bound in the slot's associated data. A modified file that carries another salt makes the client ask the authenticator for a different output, and the slot then fails to open: the commitment and the AEAD reject it. Changing the salt can only make unlock fail; it cannot make the client reveal or derive anything useful to the one who changed it.
* **Confirmed from CTAP 2.1 section 12.5 (`hmac-secret`):** the authenticator keeps two 32-byte credential secrets per credential, one used when the assertion is user-verified and one when it is not, selected by the UV bit; the platform sends one or two 32-byte salts, encrypted under the PIN/UV shared secret; the output is the HMAC-SHA-256 of the salt under the selected secret, returned encrypted. Someone holding the token without the PIN can obtain at most the other secret, which opens nothing.
* **Enforcement is in the authenticator, not the client.** A client check that refuses an assertion lacking the UV flag is not enforcement, because an attacker runs their own client and asks for the non-UV secret. The slot therefore requires the `credProtect` extension at level 3 (user verification required, for every assertion) and an authenticator whose `getInfo` reports `FIDO_2_1`. An authenticator that reports only `FIDO_2_1_PRE` is accepted if and only if enrolment confirms `credProtect` = 3 in the `makeCredential` output; it is refused otherwise. `FIDO_2_1_PRE` is what some authenticators report before they claim the final CTAP 2.1 (YubiKey firmware before 5.7 is believed to be among them; unconfirmed), and refusing it outright would exclude them although the enforcement that matters is `credProtect`. Enrolment refuses an authenticator that does not return `credProtect` = 3, whatever version it reports. The client's UV-flag check stays as a second line.
* **Does not apply:** attestation (no one decides whether to trust the authenticator model), assertion signature verification and the signature counter (no relying party), origins and `clientDataJSON`, user handles, federation. The assertion signature may be checked or ignored; the secret is the output.
* **Passkeys and browsers.** The WebAuthn Level 3 `prf` extension (section 10.1.4) is the browser name for `hmac-secret`. Its salt is SHA-256 over "WebAuthn PRF", a zero byte and the caller's input (confirmed through MDN; the specification body was not read). A relying-party id of the form `spo.<hex>` cannot be asserted from a browser: a browser accepts only a registrable domain suffix of the page's origin or `localhost`. A credential enrolled from the command line with that id is therefore unusable from a browser, and the reverse. A browser-usable slot would be a separate credential with its own id (for example `localhost` and a loopback page the user opens, which is a new surface), enrolled as its own slot. It is not in the first version. Whether a platform or synced passkey (iCloud Keychain, Google Password Manager, a password manager) offers `prf` stably across devices and keeps the secret out of the sync provider's reach varies and is unconfirmed. Roaming authenticators over USB or NFC are the supported FIDO2 slot. Synced passkeys: open questions.
* **Two authenticators at least under `hardware`.** Enrolment asks for a second one for the recovery composite of decision 6. Under `standard` a recovery key exists and a second authenticator is advised.

### 5. Vault and KMS backends, held by the client

A backend is one *factor* inside a `composite` slot, never a slot of its own. The backend wraps a random 32-byte secret B stored in the slot, and the credential for it belongs to the client: the operator's own token, OIDC or JWT identity, or cloud role, obtained on the client machine. It is never stored in the workspace and never given to a server (R4). The port is `ports.KeyWrapBackend{Wrap, Unwrap}` with a context that binds the **genesis nonce** and the slot id (not the workspace id, for the reason in decision 4): the AWS KMS encryption context `{"spo-genesis": <nonce hex>, "spo-slot": <slot id hex>}`; for Vault Transit the `associated_data` parameter on non-derived keys (supported for aes-gcm and chacha20-poly1305 keys), not `context`, which applies to derived keys, carrying the canonical encoding of `[nonce, slot id]`. B is a factor output in the derivation of decision 3, combined in the composite with at least one other factor that the backend does not control (a passphrase or a FIDO2 factor). It is never combined with "an existing slot's key": the other factor is its own factor, with its own id. A backend operator who can decrypt B holds half; a stolen laptop without backend access holds the other half.

### 6. Policy: the weakest slot is the strength of the workspace

ADR 0003 mandates one passphrase slot and one recovery slot. Each slot opens the master key alone, so a FIDO2 or Vault slot added beside them only adds another way in, and a password still opens the store. The effective strength of a workspace is the strength of its weakest slot, and the unlock policy therefore constrains which slots may exist.

**Amendment to ADR 0003** (see Amends). A workspace has a policy stored in the slot table.

* **`standard`**: at least one passphrase slot and one recovery slot, as before. A composite slot adds no strength under this policy, because a weaker slot (the passphrase or recovery slot alone) already opens the store. A FIDO2 slot under `standard` is a convenience, not a strengthening, and the slot listing says so. **A `standard` workspace should stay on the operator's machine.** Whoever holds a copy of the file can guess the passphrase offline, bounded only by the Argon2id parameters; a filesystem backup of a `standard` workspace prints a warning that names the offline-guessing exposure. A `standard` workspace is password-only and so does not meet R3.
* **`hardware`**: every slot requires a hardware factor (FIDO2). No slot opens the store from a passphrase alone, and none from the recovery key alone. A passphrase appears only inside a `composite` slot with a hardware factor. The recovery slot is **composite by default: the recovery key AND a second hardware authenticator**, enrolled for that slot only (its credential has no standalone slot) and kept apart from the daily one. A printed recovery key found by itself opens nothing. The standalone paper recovery key that the previous wording allowed is not permitted under `hardware`; the policy is named for what it guarantees, and it guarantees that nothing opens without a hardware authenticator. The cost is two authenticators.
  * **What `hardware` requires beyond slots.** A Vault or KMS backend is accepted only together with a FIDO2 factor in a `composite` slot; Vault with a passphrase is not `hardware`.
* **`strict`**: as `hardware`, with no recovery slot at all. It is an explicit opt-in with a typed warning that loss of the factors loses the workspace.

Slot add and policy change are checked in one function, and the opener refuses a slot table that violates its own policy. **Rekey** is bound by the same function (see Amends): it re-creates the slots the policy allows, each needing its factors present.

* **OR** is several slots, each wrapping the master key independently, each of them allowed by the policy.
* **AND** is a `composite` slot, derived as in decision 3. The master key is wrapped once, under that key. A factor used inside a composite must not also exist as a standalone slot (checked by factor id; two different passphrases typed identically cannot be told apart, so this holds for hardware and backend factors and is advice for passphrases).
* **N of M** with N and M small is the list of AND combinations as slots (2 of 3 is three composite slots). Threshold secret sharing is not used: it is not an IETF or NIST standard, which R5 excludes.
* The slot table, with its policy, is recorded in the audit chain (ADR 0003). A slot cannot be added by anyone who cannot already open the store, because adding a slot needs the master key.

**Removing a factor is not revocation, and it is not a rekey.** Old slot tables and ciphertext persist in backups, file-system snapshots and any copy a removed party made; a hostile holder of such a copy is assumed. A removed factor plus any old copy reads everything the store held up to that point. ADR 0003's rekey does not help: it re-wraps data keys under a new master key but does not rotate data keys or re-encrypt payloads, and an old copy with an old credential still opens everything it held. Removing a factor or a slot therefore needs a different operation, defined here.

**Rotation.** A rotation commits atomically (one SQLite transaction; an object store, per its own record). It:

1. draws a new master key and a new key epoch, and derives every purpose key anew (record wrapping, lookup index, audit, handle keys);
2. draws a **fresh data key for every record** and re-encrypts **every payload** with a fresh nonce, re-wraps and re-seals the owner blobs, and recomputes every lookup tag and every blinded handle ([ADR 0010](./0010-object-identity.md) decision 5); for a cold-role record this needs its sub-key factor present (below);
3. creates new slots without the removed factor, under the policy and with every factor of the new slots present. Re-creating the recovery composite needs the recovery key and the second authenticator present. The new recovery key is shown as ADR 0003's rekey shows it: returned as soon as the commit has succeeded, and, when the commit outcome is unknown, shown anyway, marked valid only if the change took effect, with instructions for finding out by reopening;
4. writes the new slot table and appends a rollover audit row recording the old audit chain's last row, as ADR 0003's rekey does;
5. commits atomically (one SQLite transaction; an object store, per its own record).

Old records and slot tables are replaced by the commit. **What cannot be deleted:** backups already taken, file-system snapshots, remnants on SSDs and copy-on-write file systems, and any copy that the removed party already holds. A rotation protects only what is written afterwards and what an attacker without an old copy can reach. Any key material that was exposed to the removed factor is to be rotated by the operator as well (KES, VRF and hot keys, tokens, party identity; payment, stake and cold keys cannot be rotated without moving funds or re-registering, so the tool lists them). Recommendation: cold keys live on hardware wallets, or in an offline workspace that is never placed in a shared location or sent to a server.

**Cold-role sub-keys.** A vault-held key of a cold role (pool cold, committee cold, and any role the operator marks) is sealed under a per-record sub-key in addition to the record-wrapping key. The sub-key applies by identity, not by record: every record whose key identity has a cold role in the role history ([ADR 0010](./0010-object-identity.md) decision 1) is sealed under the sub-key scheme, whatever role that record was created for. Recording a cold role for an identity that already has records re-wraps them in the same transaction, which needs the sub-key factor present; otherwise the operation is refused and the records are named. For each such record: the record's data key is wrapped a second time under the sub-key, so the master key alone, in an unlocked client or agent, opens no cold-role payload. The sub-key is derived for each signing session from a fresh factor presentation:

```
sub-key = HKDF-SHA-256(IKM  = factor output for this session,
                       salt = genesis nonce (16 bytes),
                       info = uint32_be(format version) || "cold-subkey" || record identity)
```

The record identity is the identity of ADR 0010 (a Blake2b-224 or Blake2b-256 hash) with a one-byte length prefix. The factor output depends on the policy:

* **`standard`**: a sub-key passphrase, entered again, through Argon2id with a per-record 16-byte salt stored in the record's header, with the parameters and limits of ADR 0003. The salt is rotated on each successful use: after the data key is unwrapped, the client draws a new salt, derives the next output from the passphrase it still holds in guarded memory, re-wraps the data key under the new sub-key and commits both in one transaction. **What this does and does not give.** Old copies of the wrap persist in backups and file-system snapshots, so rotating the salt protects only where no older wrap survives; it is not a protection against someone who holds an older copy and a captured output for it. What the sub-key gives under `standard` is that the bar rises from "the master key" to "the master key plus the sub-key passphrase, or an Argon2id search for it". The sub-key passphrase must differ from every slot passphrase, because an agent that was unlocked with a slot passphrase has seen it. This is enforced when the sub-key passphrase is set, by deriving the verifier of each standalone `passphrase` slot under its own salt and parameters and refusing when the new passphrase opens one. The check cannot test a passphrase that is a factor inside a `composite` slot: that slot opens only with all its factors, so a trial passphrase alone opens nothing and a match is invisible. For those passphrases the distinctness is advice, as it is for two passphrases typed identically.
* **`hardware`**: a FIDO2 `hmac-secret` output with a per-use salt. The record stores a current salt and a next salt; one assertion with both salts (CTAP 2.1 allows two) returns the output for the current salt, which unwraps, and the output for the next salt, which re-wraps the data key at the end of the session; a fresh next salt is then stored. User verification and user presence (a touch) are required (decision 4; `ports.FactorSource`, decision 8).
* **A Vault or KMS composite**: the IKM is the Vault-unwrapped secret AND the client factor (a passphrase or FIDO2 output), combined in the order and with the length prefixes of the composite slot (decision 3), so a backend operator alone and a stolen client alone both open nothing.

**Recovery of cold-role records.** The recovery composite of a `hardware` workspace, or the recovery slot of a `standard` one, opens the master key but, by the design above, no cold-role record. So each cold-role record's data key is, at creation, additionally wrapped (HPKE, RFC 9180, to an X25519 recovery public key) for the recovery path; the matching private key exists only inside the recovery slot or composite, so an unlocked agent cannot use it. Recovery unwraps each data key with that private key and wraps it again under the new sub-key factors. The recovery wrap is opened only by the recovery path and never in a signing session, so the sub-key path keeps "a fresh factor per signing session"; and under `hardware` the recovery private key is derived only from the recovery composite, so the HPKE (RFC 9180) wrap stays composite.

**The recovery public key is bound, not merely stored.** The recovery public key in the slot table could be replaced by anyone who holds the master key, and every later cold key would then be wrapped to the attacker. The SQLite store has no signed heads, and the audit chain's MAC key derives from the master key, so neither binds the recovery public key against a master-key holder. Instead the recovery public key is derived from the recovery secret, and **the hash of the recovery public key is bound in the associated data of every slot**. A client compares the key it is about to wrap to against the value bound in the slot it unlocked through on every cold-record creation, and refuses on a difference, or when the slots of the table bind different values. A rotation compares the value against the recovery composite it is presented with (the recovery key and, under `hardware`, the second authenticator); the bound value changes only in a rotation that presents that composite. **A master-key holder cannot change the bound value of an existing slot**: rewrapping a slot under changed associated data needs the slot key, which derives from factor outputs (the authenticator's `hmac-secret`, the passphrase, the backend secret) that the master key does not give. It can add a new slot binding another value, but only the holder of that slot's factors can unlock through it, and a table whose slots disagree is refused for cold-record creation and reported. Under `strict` there is no recovery wrap and a lost sub-key factor loses the record. A rotation needs the sub-key factor of every cold-role record, or the recovery path, present; otherwise it is refused and the records are named. This changes the sealed format of cold-role records and belongs to the group that needs the owner's approval of this record (decision 10).

### 7. Adopt age or HPKE for slots? No; keep the ADR 0003 format

* **age** (`age-encryption.org/v1`, c2sp.org/age): a header of stanzas wrapping a 128-bit file key, a header MAC, then a chunked payload. The scrypt passphrase stanza must be the only stanza in a header (stated in the format), so a passphrase and any other method cannot coexist in one file. No stanza composes two factors. The passphrase KDF is scrypt, not the Argon2id of ADR 0003. FIDO2 is available through the plugin protocol (c2sp.org/age-plugin, state machines `recipient-v1` and `identity-v1`, plugin binaries named `age-plugin-NAME`); the plugin's stanza format is the plugin's own, and the one FIDO2 plugin found, `olastor/age-plugin-fido2-hmac`, calls itself experimental before 1.0 and is built on libfido2 through cgo.
* **HPKE** (RFC 9180) wraps to a public key. Our factors are symmetric secrets, so it reduces to HKDF plus an AEAD again. It fits a different feature: a *device slot*, where the master key is sealed to another client's X25519 public key so that an enrolled client opens the store without a typed secret. That is not decided here; as a slot it would be subject to the policy of decision 6. `filippo.io/hpke` is already an indirect module (`spo/go.mod`).
* **JWE** (RFC 7516, general serialisation) and **COSE_Encrypt** (RFC 9052) have multiple recipients, which is OR, but no AND of factors and no Argon2id passphrase mode (the COSE registries were not searched for a later addition; unconfirmed).
* **Honest comparison.** R5 is better met by age's wider adoption, but not by a format that cannot hold two methods. The ADR 0003 container is bespoke and stays, because no standard container holds an AND of factors with an Argon2id passphrase and a hardware secret, and because its primitives are standard (XChaCha20-Poly1305, Argon2id, HKDF-SHA256, RFC 5869 derivation as in decision 3), it has committed vectors and seven reviews. age stays where it is used (ADR 0005 envelopes and exports).

### 8. Libraries for CTAP2

| Option | Facts | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| libfido2 through cgo (for example `keys-pub/go-libfido2`, which the age FIDO2 plugin uses) | Mature C library, hmac-secret supported | Needs cgo, which `tools/spo.nix` turns off (`env.CGO_ENABLED = 0`) and ADR 0002 lists as a consequence (static, no CGO); a static link needs libfido2, libcbor, OpenSSL and hidapi or udev built for it |
| Pure Go `go-ctap/ctaphid` and `go-ctap/ctap` | hmac-secret listed; "work in progress, API may change"; cgo only for the macOS HID transport (from the project pages); hmac-secret-mc untested on hardware | Keeps the static binary on Linux; macOS needs cgo; young, unaudited |
| The libfido2 command-line tools (`fido2-token`, `fido2-assert`) driven through `execx`, pinned in the toolchain manifest | The same pattern as gpg and cardano-hw-cli (D2); secrets through inherited file descriptors | No cgo, no new Go module; depends on the tools' hmac-secret options, which I did not confirm; an extra pinned package in `tools/spo.nix` |

Recommendation: define `ports.FactorSource` and drive the libfido2 command-line tools first, secrets through file descriptors. Its shape is fixed here so that it is not rebuilt: an **enrol** operation that makes the credential (relying-party id, user verification, `hmac-secret` and `credProtect` requested) and returns the credential id, the `credProtect` level the authenticator confirmed, and the versions it reported in `getInfo`; and an **assertion** operation that takes the credential id, the relying-party id and two 32-byte salts (the current and the next, as `hmac-secret` allows) and returns the two 32-byte outputs (the second is absent when no next salt is given) together with the user-presence (UP) and user-verification (UV) flags of the assertion. Enrolment defines the **factor id** (a value the port derives from the credential id and stores in the slot, used in the ordering and binding of decision 3) and returns it. Both operations take a context that carries the presence and user-verification requirement; the sub-key assertions of decision 6 require user presence, and the client refuses an assertion whose UP flag is not set. The first implementation is a stand-in (decision 9). The port and the stand-in are buildable now (decision 10) because they freeze no slot format. No pure-Go CTAP library on the unlock path before an audit (open questions). Each choice is confined to one adapter and added to the `confined` table in `spo/internal/archtest/arch_test.go`. The Vault client is a small HTTP adapter (as `koios` is), not the Vault SDK. AWS KMS needs the AWS SDK's KMS module; `go.mod` lists only config, credentials, STS, SSO and SSM of that SDK as indirect, so KMS is a new module (unconfirmed which).

**Limit.** A compromised client that has seen the PIN can obtain an `hmac-secret` output without a touch when the authenticator allows an assertion with `up=false` and a cached PIN token. The touch requirement is therefore enforced by the authenticator only where it supports that. Enrolment checks it: it makes a test assertion with `up=false` using a PIN token and records whether the authenticator returns an output; the result is stored with the factor, an authenticator that returns one is marked "touch not enforced" and shown so in the slot listing. For such an authenticator the touch requirement of the sub-key is a stated residual risk and not a guarantee. Which authenticators enforce it, and the exact `getInfo` options that report it, are unconfirmed.

### 9. Testing without authenticators

What needs real devices: user-verification behaviour of firmware, `credProtect` enforcement, PIN retry counters and lock-out, touch timeouts, the separation of the two hmac-secret secrets on real silicon, USB permissions (udev), NFC. The stand-in follows `test/hardware/fakehw`: a program driven by a JSON file that implements `hmac-secret` as an HMAC-SHA-256 over the salt under a seed-derived credential secret per UV state, logs every invocation's argument vector, and has `misbehave` modes (UV flag missing, the other secret returned, credProtect not 3, wrong credential, no extension, timeout). Vault is tested against a dev-mode Vault in CI or a recorded-response fake of Transit; policy denials and token expiry need a real one. Real-device checks are a manual list in the handoff, as for the hardware wallets.

## Consequences

* The release binary has one writer of a signing key in clear, the block producer's start-up helper, subject to the owner; the host that receives the `node export` ciphertext holds the decryption identity in a TPM-bound credential.
* Two commands, an export mode and a flag disappear; the parity suite moves to a test package and a tag. A one-time migration for operators of the plain-directory store is `workspace import`.
* New slot kinds need new test vectors, a format version, and a slot-listing UI in the CLI and TUI. Enrolment of a factor is a rekey-class operation in the audit chain; removal is a rotation.
* A hardware-policy workspace cannot be opened by a password alone or by the recovery key alone, but a copy of the store from before a factor was removed stays readable with that factor.
* A forgotten FIDO2 PIN with no second authenticator and no recovery composite is unrecoverable. The `strict` mode makes that a choice.
* An unlock needs the client's own backend credential; unattended unlock does not exist.
* Rekey needs every factor of the slots it re-creates to be present, so a rekey with a lost authenticator drops that slot.
* A `standard` workspace is password-only, does not meet R3, and should stay on the operator's machine.
* A cold-role key needs a fresh factor for every signing session and the master key is not enough to read it; the cost is one more presentation and one more wrap per cold-role record.

## Amendments

Accepted by the owner, 2026-11-18. These record what the implementation does where the decisions were silent or were changed.

* **Rekey under `hardware` and `strict` is refused.** The Amends entry for ADR 0003 "Rekey" step 3 says a rekey "re-creates the slots that the workspace policy allows". The implementation refuses a rekey there and names `spo workspace rotate`, because a rekey makes a passphrase slot and a recovery slot.
* **`strict` is never loosened.** The ADR does not say how a strict workspace may become `hardware` or `standard`; it has no recovery credential that could authorise it, so a rotation to a weaker policy is refused.
* **Every rotation or rekey of a workspace with a recovery path needs the current recovery credential.** A new recovery key replaces the recovery public key hash bound in every slot, and decision 6 says that value changes only in a rotation that presents the recovery composite. Under `standard` a passphrase change therefore presents the recovery key and keeps it; a new recovery key is made only with the current one presented, or in `store recover`. This is an in-process check: the cryptographic guarantee is only that an existing slot's bound hash cannot change without that slot's factors. A holder of the daily factor who runs their own code can re-create their own slot and drop the recovery slot; that is detectable (the recovery key stops opening) but not preventable until cold-record recovery wraps consume the hash.
* **Co-holder removal.** A `strict` rotation by one daily factor removes the other holders' slots. The implementation names each removed slot and requires `--yes-remove <slot id>` for each. Whether a factor of each removed slot should be required instead is open.
* **Kept FIDO2 factors get a fresh salt** in a rotation or a removal, so a captured hmac-secret output does not survive it.
* **HPKE parameters for the cold-record recovery wrap (built, as proposed).** RFC 9180 base mode, DHKEM(X25519, HKDF-SHA256) / HKDF-SHA256 / ChaCha20-Poly1305; `info` = canonical CBOR `["spo/cold-recovery-wrap/1", format version, genesis nonce, recovery public key hash, record identity]`; AAD = the record's associated data, including its version and class; the key epoch is NOT bound, so a rotation does not invalidate wraps held for recovery; on recovery the decrypted signing key must derive the record's stored identity before it is re-wrapped.
* **`node export` and the start-up helper are built** as decision 2 describes (age ciphertext to the producer's recipient key; `spo-node-keys` is a separate program, the only plaintext writer, which cannot open a workspace). Where the text is silent the implementation takes the most restrictive reading, and the owner may relax each: the helper's identity is read only from the systemd credential `spo-node-age-identity` in `$CREDENTIALS_DIRECTORY` (plain file, no group or other bits, on ramfs); the helper refuses to run as root (decision 2 says "0400 for the node user", read as: owned by the user it runs as); the helper refuses a directory that is not ramfs for the credentials as well as for the output (decision 2 says whether systemd's credentials directory is ramfs or tmpfs is unconfirmed); the helper never replaces a file; the recipient is given as a file of one X25519 key; `node export` cannot see the producer's host, so "records that the host has no TPM-bound identity" is read as a record of `not verified`, or `none` when the operator says so with `--no-tpm-identity`. A kept-encrypted KES or VRF key can now be converted by `key convert`, because the native deriver derives both.
* **Node delivery, later changes.** The start-up helper is a separate program in its own package (`spo-node-keys`) that imports only `adapters/native/hotkeys`, not `adapters/native`. `tmpfs` is accepted for the output and credentials directories only when the mount carrying the directory has `noswap` (decision 2 refused tmpfs outright because its pages can be swapped; `noswap` keeps them out of swap); ramfs is accepted as before. Each delivered file carries a header (pool id, KES number, certificate counter) and the helper refuses a mixed set; this is not sender authentication, and the helper keeps no state, so it does not refuse an older complete set (an owner decision). `node export` records `tpm-identity=not verified` by default because it cannot see the host. The operational certificate is verified at export and by the helper. Status: accepted, 2026-11-18.
* **Cold-role sub-keys and the recovery wrap: built; the choices made where the text is silent.** Each is the most restrictive reading and the owner may relax it. (1) "the record's data key is wrapped a second time under the sub-key": read as nested, not parallel. The stored wrapped key is the data key sealed under the sub-key (inner), and that is sealed under the record-wrapping key (outer); a parallel wrap would leave the master key a way in. (2) "Recording a cold role for an identity that already has records re-wraps them in the same transaction": the re-seal is a full re-seal of the payload (fresh data key) in the same transaction as the role-history record, refused with nothing changed when the factor is absent. (3) The record identity in the sub-key `info` and in the HPKE `info` is the hash bytes of the key identity (28 or 32 bytes), with the one-byte length prefix only in the sub-key's `info`, as the ADR says. (4) "Classification" needs a key identity for a signing key, and the ADR does not say where the store finds it: it is the identity of the verification key stored with the same anchor and number, and a signing key with no verification key beside it is refused (a store write order the use cases did not need before). Calidus and Catalyst voting verification keys, and verification keys of an owner with no anchoring key yet, now record a role history when written, because the store refuses a signing key whose identity has none. (5) Under `standard` the ADR describes the per-record salt rotating on each successful use; the sub-key passphrase is asked once per operation and a fresh salt is derived from it, in the transaction that releases the secret, so the record's version rises with every open. (6) Under `hardware` and `strict` the "fresh hardware factor presentation" is the daily authenticator of the slot the handle unlocked through; the credential is stored in the record's header and only that credential opens the record. The ADR does not say which credential when a workspace has several daily slots. (7) The recovery public key is stored in `meta.recovery_pub` (a column added to the format 3 migration text) and every cold put and open compares its hash with the value the slots bind, refusing on a difference; the ADR says the client compares "the key it is about to wrap to" but not where the key is kept. (8) The SHA-256 of the recovery wrap is in the associated data of the outer layer, so a master-key holder who swaps a wrap must re-seal the outer layer; a holder of the master key can still do that, and the recovery identity check is what refuses a planted wrap. (9) A rotation seals every cold record again under the new factor. It opens a record that has a recovery wrap through the wrap, with the recovery credential presented and the identity check, whatever cold source the handle has wired; it opens a record that has no wrap, whose key cannot be checked against its identity, or whose identity check failed, with the old factor (a record that opens with the operator's own old factor is accepted; a master-key holder who plants a wrap cannot produce that inner wrap); and it refuses, before anything is written and naming the records, when neither is possible. This is the rule the accepted text states for recording a cold role ("otherwise the operation is refused and the records are named"), applied to a rotation and to a recovery; the text is silent on a rotation. The only exception is an explicit opt-in, `--leave-unreachable kind/label` per record, honoured only when the recovery credential was presented: the record keeps its old factor, a `cold-left` event naming it is written in the same transaction, and a left mark is set in the record's cold form (outside the inner wrap, inside the outer layer's associated data), so `store verify` fails and `store slots` lists it, without any factor and through every later rekey, until a rotation re-establishes it. A left record is not compared when a new cold passphrase is checked. (10) A rekey that makes a new recovery key moves the recovery wraps to the new key and needs the current recovery credential presented, which decision 6 requires of any change of the bound value. A record made before a workspace bound a recovery key (a format 2 migration) has no wrap until the first rekey that binds one (see the third-pass entry below). (11) A migration from format 2 seals cold keys under a sub-key passphrase it asks for and creates: this is "re-wraps them ... which needs the sub-key factor present", with the factor created at that moment. (12) Deep verification of a cold record opens only the outer layer (the master key) and says how many it could not open; the factor variant is `store verify --deep --with-cold-factor`. (13) Under `standard`, a sub-key passphrase typed at the creation of a second cold record must open an existing passphrase-sealed cold record: the outer layer opens with the master key, so the store tries the offered passphrase on the inner wrap of every existing passphrase-sealed record (one Argon2id run each) and refuses a mismatch (`ErrColdPassphraseDiffers`); a passphrase that opens some of them and not others is reported as a split workspace (`ErrColdSplit`, which names `store verify`). A workspace has one cold passphrase; the decision says nothing about a second one, and this is the most restrictive reading. Changing it is done by rotation. (The first reading of this amendment said the store could not tell; it can.) Status: accepted, 2026-11-18.
* **Recovery material has a lifetime of one operation.** Decision 6 says the recovery private key "exists only inside the recovery slot or composite, so an unlocked agent cannot use it" and that the bound hash changes "only in a rotation that presents that composite". A handle that kept the proof, or the recovery secret of an unlock through the recovery credential, after the operation would let a process that holds the master key derive the recovery private key and open every cold record through its wrap. The implementation destroys the proof and the recovery secret at the end of the rekey, rotation or slot removal it was presented for, on success and on failure, and on every rekey; a handle opened through recovery is good for the recovery operation and nothing else. Closing the handle destroys them as before. Not stated by the ADR; most restrictive reading.
* **The mnemonic of a cold role is a cold record.** Decision 6 classifies "a vault-held key of a cold role". The words of `cc-cold.mnemonics` derive the committee cold key, so the record is sealed in the cold form, bound to the identity of the verification key beside it, and refused when there is none. Mnemonic kinds examined: `cc-cold.mnemonics` (derives the committee cold key: cold); `payment`, `drep`, `cc-hot`, `calidus` and `voting` mnemonics (derive payment/stake, DRep, committee hot, Calidus and Catalyst voting keys: no cold role; hot). There is no pool cold mnemonic kind. A phrase reused by the operator across kinds is not detectable by the store. A cold mnemonic is rotated through the recovery wrap like any other cold record: the recovered words are checked by deriving the committee cold verification key with the generator's derivation at account 0, index 0 (a narrow port, `ports.ColdMnemonicVKeyDeriver`, implemented beside the wallet derivation in `adapters/native`) and comparing its identity with that of the signing key beside it; a mismatch is refused as tampering (`ErrRecoveredIdentity`). The ADR and the generator's spec are silent on recording the path, so the generator now records it in the sealed record (a second line, `derivation <account> <index>`, written only when not 0, 0; the store releases the words only) and the check uses it. A record without it is checked at 0, 0 and, on a mismatch, falls back to the old factor, else is named and refused unless left.
* **`--defer-encrypted` is built as decision 2 words it; `--keep-encrypted` is removed** and refused with a message naming the new flag. Existing `UpstreamEncryptedSKey` records are sealed in the cold form when their key identity (that of the verification key beside them) has a cold role; one whose stem is a cold kind and that has no verification key beside it is refused (the record would be quarantined with no identity). `key convert` opens such a record first, so it needs the cold factor, and asks again for the key it writes.
* **Envelope type and record kind.** A signing-key envelope of a cold type (`StakePool*`, `ConstitutionalCommitteeCold*`) is refused under any record kind but its own, at put and at import. The opposite (another type under a cold kind) is not refused, because the types of the other roles are not all known; such a record is sealed cold by its kind.
* **No cold record through the recovery slot.** A handle unlocked through a slot that holds the recovery factor creates no cold record and re-binds none to the recovery authenticator. Decision 6 does not say; most restrictive reading.
* **The HPKE `info` and the sub-key `info` bind the version of the cold record form** ("spo/cold-record/1", `seal.ColdRecordFormat`), not the store format, so a later store format does not strand cold records. `info` of the HPKE wrap was written in the amendment above with the store format; it is the cold record format.
* **A key that gains a cold role truncates the write-ahead log** at the end of the transaction that re-sealed its records, as a rotation does.
* **Third pass: recovery wraps for migrated records, the previous wrap, the factor outside the lock.** Where the text is silent the most restrictive reading is taken and the owner may relax it. (1) "Recovery of cold-role records" says the wrap is made when a record is sealed, and says nothing about records sealed before the table bound a recovery key. A rekey, passphrase change or rotation that leaves the table binding a recovery hash adds a wrap to every cold record that lacks one; it needs the cold factor once per record, presented before the write transaction (and before the exclusive file lock), and is refused with nothing changed when the factor is absent or wrong. `workspace migrate` ends with a warning that names `spo store passphrase change`; `store slots` and `store verify` report "N cold records without a recovery wrap", read with the workspace key (the wrap is authenticated by the outer layer), never under `strict`. `store recover` now wires the cold source because its rekey needs it; the owner may prefer that a recovery of a workspace whose cold passphrase is lost goes on and leaves those keys without a wrap. (2) Decision 6 says the per-record salt rotates after use and that under hardware the next salt's output re-wraps the data key; it does not say what happens when the authenticator answers the next salt wrongly, which nothing can check, and under `strict` there is no recovery wrap to fall back on. The cold record keeps the previous inner wrap (the salt just proved good) beside the current one, both inside the outer layer; an open tries the current, then the previous; the previous is replaced only by an open that succeeded with the current. At most one is kept. Cost: a captured output for the previous salt opens the record for one more rotation. Under `standard` the next salt is derived in process, so no previous wrap is kept. The header of an hmac-secret record gains a fifth element (the previous salt); format 3 is unreleased, so the encoding changed in place. (3) Decision 6 says the factor is presented fresh; it does not say when relative to the write. A new cold record's factor is gathered before the write transaction (a first attempt that counts the cold records and is rolled back, then the factor, then the real attempt that only consumes it), never under the lock; a write that needs more than was gathered fails and asks nothing. The ADR's "the command declares it" is satisfied by the store learning the need from the write itself, so no command can forget to declare. (4) The same-passphrase check of a second cold record skips a row that does not authenticate and refuses when cold rows exist and none can be used. Not decided here: a new hardware cold record is sealed under an authenticator answer nothing verifies at creation (the unlock proved the authenticator, not this answer).
* **Fifth review: recovery, the verified fallback, two touches.** The ADR's recovery paragraph says "Recovery unwraps each data key with that private key and wraps it again under the new sub-key factors". The implementation follows it: with the recovery credential present, a cold record that has a wrap is opened through the wrap (identity check) and never through the old factor, whatever cold source is wired; `store recover` re-establishes the records under a new cold passphrase; a record that needs its old factor (no wrap, or an upstream-encrypted key) and cannot get it REFUSES the recovery with the record named, unless the operator names it in `--leave-unreachable` (this corrects the first reading, which skipped such a record with a notice and went on, and left a hardware workspace holding a cold record that nothing could open). Under `hardware` the documented steps are the composite session and `workspace rotate --new-fido2-device`; `store recover` refuses on a `hardware` or `strict` workspace and names them. Every record the old factor opens is sealed again under the new factor, so a recovery never leaves two cold passphrases. No slot is created on a handle opened through a slot that holds the recovery factor, and a credential of the recovery composite is refused as a daily factor; the accepted text says "A slot cannot be added by anyone who cannot already open the store" and is silent on a handle opened through the recovery credential, so the most restrictive reading is taken. A cold record opens with the factor its own header names, not the one the workspace policy implies. Awaiting the owner's confirmation: a recovery may name the OLD recovery token again as the new `--recovery-device` (it presents the composite and enrols a new credential on it); the accepted text does not say whether the recovery authenticator may be reused. A fallback open verifies the new current salt by a second assertion; on disagreement nothing is written and the operator is warned each time; the header counts heals. Each new hardware salt (creation, rotation) is asserted twice and compared. These add touches; the ADR is silent on the number of touches. The earlier sentence that a handle opened through recovery "is good for the recovery operation and nothing else" is corrected: only the guarded operations (rekey, rotation, slot removal) are blocked once the presentation is consumed; ordinary reads and writes on that handle still work.
* **Not built.** Decision 6's "any role the operator marks": the cold roles are fixed at pool cold and committee cold. The Vault or KMS composite sub-key: `DeriveColdSubKeyComposite` exists and is not used by the store or any command.
